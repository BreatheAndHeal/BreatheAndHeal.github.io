DIFFICULT CONVERSATIONS

How to Have a Difficult Conversation

You know there is something you need to say, but every possible version sounds too harsh, too emotional, too awkward—or like it might start a fight. Difficult conversations rarely need perfect words. They usually need a clear intention, a little preparation and enough honesty to say what actually matters.

A practical reflection guide from The Unsaid. This page is for general information and reflection, not diagnosis or professional counselling.

Why do I keep avoiding the conversation?

Avoiding a difficult conversation can feel like keeping the peace, especially when you are worried about rejection, anger, disappointment or being misunderstood.

But avoidance can also leave the issue running silently in the background. The longer you rehearse every possible reaction in your head, the harder it can become to find a simple opening.

You do not have to feel completely confident before you speak. Confidence can come after you have started.

What should I say when I don't know how to start?

Start with three sentences

  1. What is happening: “There is something I've been meaning to talk about.”
  2. What it feels like for you: “I've been feeling ___ when ___ happens.”
  3. What you need: “What I need/want is ___.”

You do not need a ten-minute explanation before the important sentence. Start with enough context for the other person to understand what you are talking about, then let the conversation develop.

The NHS recommends planning what you want to say, writing down a few thoughts beforehand if useful, and telling the other person what kind of support or conversation you need. NHS: How to talk about your mental health.

How do I tell someone how I really feel?

Use language that describes your experience rather than trying to prove the other person is a bad person.

Compare:

  • “You never care about me.”
  • “I've been feeling like I am carrying this alone, and I need more support.”

The second sentence does not guarantee a better response, but it gives the other person something specific to understand.

How do I set a boundary without feeling guilty?

A boundary is not a way to control another person's behaviour. It is a clear statement about what you are willing to do, accept or participate in.

Try: “I am not comfortable with that. I can do X, but I cannot do Y.”

You do not need to build a courtroom case for every boundary. Repeating the same clear sentence can be more useful than adding five new explanations every time the other person argues.

Current NHS communication guidance also highlights setting boundaries, taking time for yourself and talking to someone you trust when relationships become stressful. NHS: Healthy communication and boundaries.

What if I'm scared they'll get angry?

You can prepare for an uncomfortable response without assuming the conversation will go badly.

Before you start, decide what you will do if the conversation becomes heated: pause, take some time out, return to it later, or end the conversation for the moment. You are allowed to say, “I want to talk about this, but not while we are shouting at each other.”

The goal of a difficult conversation is not to control the other person's emotions. It is to communicate clearly while keeping your own behaviour within your control.

What if they disagree with me?

A successful conversation does not always end with agreement.

You may be able to understand each other's position without choosing the same conclusion. Decide beforehand what is actually negotiable and what is not. That makes it easier to listen without abandoning your own needs.

NHS guidance on relationships recommends active listening—checking that you understood what the other person meant—and taking time out if a disagreement cannot be discussed calmly. Read the NHS communication guidance.

How do I listen without losing my point?

Listening is not surrender. You can let someone finish, reflect back what you heard and still disagree.

Useful questions are:

  • “What did you hear me say?”
  • “What part of this feels unfair to you?”
  • “What do you need me to understand?”

Open questions and active listening can make difficult conversations more productive because they create space for both people to explain what they mean rather than simply defending their first reaction.

What if I need to have this conversation with my partner, family or friend?

Choose the part that actually matters. A relationship conversation can become overwhelming when you bring every old argument into the same room.

Try to identify one issue, one feeling and one request. You can always agree to have another conversation later. The NHS also recommends choosing a time when both people have enough space and avoiding turning a serious conversation into a rushed exchange. NHS: Preparing to talk.

What if I keep rewriting the message before I send it?

Sometimes drafting helps. Sometimes rewriting becomes another form of avoidance.

Write the message once. Remove insults, mind-reading and accusations you cannot prove. Keep the actual request. Then ask yourself: “Am I trying to communicate, or am I trying to guarantee the exact response I want?”

You cannot write a sentence powerful enough to control another person's reaction.

When a conversation should not happen alone or immediately

If someone has threatened you, used violence, stalked you, seriously intimidated you, or you are worried about your immediate safety, do not treat the situation as an ordinary communication problem. Your safety comes first. Consider getting support from someone you trust and an appropriate local professional or emergency service.

A calm conversation is useful when both people can participate safely. It is not a requirement when there is abuse or an immediate risk of harm.

What if the conversation changes nothing?

You can communicate clearly and still discover that the other person does not agree, cannot meet the need, or does not want to change.

That information matters. A conversation is sometimes valuable not because it fixes the relationship, but because it tells you what is actually possible from here.

Before you have the conversation, ask yourself these five questions

  1. What is the one thing I need to say?
  2. What am I feeling?
  3. What am I asking for?
  4. What am I willing to hear?
  5. What will I do if the answer is no?

You are preparing for a conversation—not writing a script for another person's behaviour.

A note from The Unsaid:

This guide is for reflection and general communication guidance. It does not diagnose relationship problems or mental-health conditions, and it is not a substitute for professional, legal or crisis support when those are needed.

If a difficult conversation is connected to abuse, threats, coercion or immediate danger, prioritise safety and seek appropriate local support rather than trying to solve the situation through conversation alone.

Sometimes the hardest sentence is the first one

You do not have to make your feelings sound perfectly reasonable before you are allowed to talk about them.

You can begin with something as simple as: “There is something I have been keeping to myself, and I think I need to say it.”

That may not solve everything. But it can finally make the real conversation possible.
