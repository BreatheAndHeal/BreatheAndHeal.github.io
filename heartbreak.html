HEARTBREAK

How to Stop Thinking About Someone After a Breakup

Someone can leave your life and still take up a surprising amount of space in your mind. You replay the conversations, check old messages, imagine different endings, or wonder whether one more conversation would change everything.

A practical reflection guide from The Unsaid. This page is for general information and reflection, not diagnosis or medical treatment.

Why can't I stop thinking about my ex?

Because a breakup is not only the loss of a person. You may also be losing a routine, a source of comfort, a future you pictured, a version of yourself, or the feeling of being chosen and understood.

That is why “just move on” can feel almost insulting when your mind is still trying to make sense of what happened. The intensity of the thoughts does not automatically mean the relationship was right for you or that you should get back together.

The American Psychological Association notes that breakups can bring loneliness, distress and a sense of losing part of your identity, while some people also experience personal growth as they adjust to the end of a relationship. Read the APA's overview of coping after breakups.

Why do I keep replaying the relationship?

Your mind may be looking for a clean explanation: What went wrong? Did I miss something? Was it my fault? What if I had said something different?

Looking back can help you learn. But there is a point where reflection becomes repetition. Ask yourself: “Am I discovering something new, or am I reopening the same scene because I want a different ending?”

Why do I want to text them even when I know I shouldn't?

The urge to reach out can be strongest when you feel lonely, uncertain or emotionally activated. In that moment, a message can feel like a quick way to reduce the discomfort.

Before you send it, pause and name what you actually want from the message:

  • an answer?
  • reassurance?
  • an apology?
  • a second chance?
  • or simply relief from missing them for the next ten minutes?

Knowing the need underneath the urge does not tell you what decision to make. It does give you a chance to make the decision consciously instead of sending the message automatically.

What about checking their social media?

A profile can feel like a tiny connection to someone you are trying to let go of. But every check can also pull your attention back into the story—what they posted, who they are with, whether they look happy, and what it might mean.

You do not have to make a dramatic promise to “never look again.” Start smaller: mute, unfollow or move the app shortcut for a while if checking is keeping you emotionally stuck.

How do I actually start moving forward?

Try this instead of forcing yourself to “get over it”

  1. Name what you miss. The person? The routine? The future you imagined? The feeling?
  2. Return to what you know. Separate what actually happened from what you wish had happened.
  3. Protect a little space. Reduce the habits that repeatedly reopen the wound—old chats, constant profile checking, late-night re-reading.
  4. Give your day somewhere else to go. See a friend, walk, work, study, exercise, write, or return to something you stopped doing.
  5. Let the feelings exist without making every feeling a decision. Missing someone does not require contacting them.

The NHS recommends talking about difficult feelings, using support from trusted people or professionals, keeping small manageable goals, and taking care of your basic routine while coping with loss. It also recognises the end of a relationship as a form of loss that can bring grief. See the NHS guidance on grief and loss.

How long does it take to get over someone?

There is no honest number of days that works for everyone. Some people feel better relatively quickly; others need much longer. Progress is rarely a straight line.

You might have a good week and then have one difficult night. That does not mean you are back at the beginning. It may simply mean the loss still has some emotional weight.

The American Psychological Association also emphasises that people respond differently to loss and that there is not a single timetable for grief. APA on coping with loss and grief.

What if I still love them?

You can love someone and still recognise that the relationship is not something you can—or should—return to. Feelings do not always disappear at the same speed as decisions.

Sometimes healing is not becoming indifferent. It is being able to remember without immediately wanting to reopen the relationship.

When heartbreak starts affecting your everyday life

Sadness after a breakup is human. But if low mood, anxiety, sleep problems or distress become persistent and are making it hard to function day after day, it is worth reaching out for qualified mental-health support.

You do not have to wait until things become unbearable before asking for help. A trusted person, counsellor, psychologist, psychiatrist or other qualified professional may be a better fit when the problem is affecting daily life.

A note from The Unsaid:

This guide is for general reflection and does not diagnose depression, anxiety or any other mental-health condition. A private conversation can give you space to organise what you are carrying, but it is not a substitute for qualified clinical care.

If you are in immediate danger or thinking about harming yourself, seek urgent local emergency or crisis support rather than relying on this page or an online session.

Sometimes you are not looking for another explanation

You may already know what happened. What you might not have is a place to say the part you keep editing before you tell anyone: the anger, the regret, the unanswered question, the hope that you are trying not to admit, or the simple sentence—“I still miss them.”

You do not need a perfect story before you start talking.
